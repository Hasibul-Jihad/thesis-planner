Month 1 — Python & ML Foundations · 44 hours
Learn to code. Understand what ML is. Do not touch audio yet.
Weeks 1–2 (22 hrs): Python fundamentals — variables, loops, functions, lists, dicts. Use Claude/ChatGPT to explain every error. Work through freeCodeCamp Python or CS50P daily. Goal: write a script that reads a CSV and prints averages.
Week 3 (11 hrs): NumPy and Pandas basics. Load data, slice arrays, compute statistics. Kaggle micro-courses are free and take ~1 hr each.
Week 4 (11 hrs): Watch 3Blue1Brown Neural Networks playlist (8 videos). Read Chapters 1–2 of the ML guide. No coding this week — understanding only.
Month 1 Checkpoint
You can write a Python script that loads data, filters it, and prints results without help.
Month 2 — Audio Signal Processing · 44 hours
Learn the language of audio. Build your first audio classifier.
Week 1 (11 hrs): Valerio Velardo — Audio Signal Processing for ML playlist, first 8 videos. Re-watch with a Colab notebook open. Plot waveforms and spectrograms in Python.
Week 2 (11 hrs): MFCCs and Mel spectrograms using librosa. Extract features from 10 audio files. Understand what each feature means physically.
Week 3 (11 hrs): HuggingFace Audio Course, Section 1 — Audio classification. Copy the tutorial exactly; understanding comes from running it.
Week 4 (11 hrs): Download ESC-50. Build a mel spectrogram + CNN classifier end to end. Doesn't need good accuracy — just working.
Month 2 Checkpoint
You can load any audio file, extract a mel spectrogram, and run it through a classifier.
Month 3 — Literature Review & Topic Lock · 44 hours
Read 5 papers. Lock your scope. Write introduction and literature review.
Week 1 (11 hrs): Read 5 papers: wav2vec 2.0 (arxiv 2006.11477), HuBERT (arxiv 2106.07447), SUBESCO dataset paper, + 2 papers on Bangla speech emotion recognition. Ask AI to explain any paragraph you don't understand.
Week 2 (11 hrs): Finalise research question with supervisor. Write thesis introduction (1–2 pages). Question: "How does fine-tuned HuBERT compare to a CNN baseline for Bangla SER?"
Week 3 (11 hrs): Write literature review (4–6 pages). Cover: speech emotion recognition, transfer learning for audio, Bangla speech processing state of the art.
Week 4 (11 hrs): Download SUBESCO dataset. Explore its structure. Write methodology chapter outline.
Month 3 Checkpoint

Research question finalised. Introduction + literature review drafted. Dataset downloaded and explored.

Month 4 — Baseline Implementation · 44 hours
Build CNN baseline. Fine-tune HuBERT. Run first experiments.
Week 1 (11 hrs): Preprocess SUBESCO: load audio, extract mel spectrograms, create 70/15/15 train/val/test splits. Write the data loading pipeline.
Week 2 (11 hrs): Build CNN baseline: mel spectrogram → 3-layer CNN → emotion class. Train on Colab free GPU. Record accuracy and F1 per emotion class.
Week 3 (11 hrs): Fine-tune HuBERT using HuggingFace Trainer API. Use the audio course fine-tuning tutorial as your exact template.
Week 4 (11 hrs): Compare CNN vs HuBERT: accuracy, F1, confusion matrix per emotion. Write methodology chapter. Produce results table.
Month 4 Checkpoint
Both models trained. Results table complete. Methodology chapter written.
Month 5 — Analysis, Ablations & Results Writing · 44 hours
Dig into results. Run extra experiments. Write results and discussion.
Week 1 (11 hrs): Error analysis: which emotions are confused? Listen to misclassified clips. Try data augmentation (pitch shift, noise) and retrain.
Week 2 (11 hrs): Ablation: what happens with 50% / 25% of training data? How does HuBERT degrade vs CNN? This is a genuine contribution at BSc level.
Weeks 3–4 (22 hrs): Write results chapter (all tables, confusion matrix figures) and discussion chapter (why does HuBERT outperform? what are the limitations?).
Month 5 Checkpoint
Results + discussion chapters complete. Thesis ~70% written.
Month 6 — Writing, Polishing & Submission · 44 hours
Write conclusion. Revise everything. Submit.
Week 1 (11 hrs): Write conclusion: what you found, why it matters, limitations, future work. Write abstract last — easiest when done.
Week 2 (11 hrs): Full readthrough — fix structure, transitions, grammar. Every figure needs a caption. Every claim needs a citation.
Week 3 (11 hrs): Supervisor review round. Apply feedback. Format references (IEEE or APA per your department).
Week 4 (11 hrs): Buffer week — final supervisor comments, viva/defence slides if required, final submission.

The Golden Rule
Phone off. One task only. No switching. 1 quality hour beats 3 distracted hours every time. Use Pomodoro: 25 min work → 5 min break → 25 min work = your 1 hour.

Thesis Chapter Completion
The Most Important Thing
Consistency beats intensity. 11 hours a week every week for 26 weeks = 286 hours of genuine work. That is more than enough. One missed week is fine. Two consecutive missed weeks = you are behind. Protect Month 1 and 2 — they have the most slack.
On using AI to help you code
Using Claude or ChatGPT to write and explain code is fine and expected at BSc level — as long as you understand every line you submit. Ask AI to write something, then ask it to explain each line. If you can explain your code to your supervisor, you know it well enough. Never copy-paste without reading.
The 3 mistakes that kill BSc theses
Scope creepTrying to build too much. ONE research question. ONE clear answer. Not five experiments.
Skip lit reviewSupervisors fail theses that ignore prior work. Month 3 is not optional.
Bulk writingLeaving all writing to Month 6. Write one paragraph every day from Month 3 onwards.
How to talk to your supervisor
Meet at least once a month. Come with: (1) what you did, (2) what result you got, (3) one specific problem you're facing. Ask one specific question. Supervisors respect students who show work even when it's wrong. They cannot help students who disappear.
When to relax vs act fast
✓ Relax ifModel doesn't converge in Week 1 · Accuracy is low at first · You don't understand a paper fully · One bug takes 3 sessions to fix. All normal.
⚡ Act fast ifNo supervisor meeting by end of Month 3 · Dataset unavailable · Zero pages written by end of Month 3 · 3+ consecutive weeks missed.

Music genre classification — CNN vs Transformer comparison
⭐ Top pick
Difficulty 2/5 GTZAN dataset Colab friendly Comparative study
Easiest — best for beginners
Classify songs into genres (rock, jazz, classical, hip-hop…) by converting audio to mel spectrograms and comparing a CNN baseline against an Audio Spectrogram Transformer. A comparative study structure is perfect for BSc — you compare two methods rigorously rather than invent something new.
Full details
DatasetGTZAN — 1000 clips, 10 genres (blues, classical, country, disco, hip-hop, jazz, metal, pop, reggae, rock), 30 sec each. Free download.
Dataset link Option 1 (easiest): huggingface.co/datasets/marsyas/gtzan — load directly in Python with datasets.load_dataset("marsyas/gtzan")

Option 2 (Kaggle): kaggle.com — GTZAN dataset — download as zip, free Kaggle account needed

Note: The original marsyas.info link has been broken since 2022. Use HuggingFace — it's the official mirror.

MetricAccuracy + F1 per genre + confusion matrix showing which genres are confused
Novel angleAblation: compare both models with 100%, 50%, and 25% training data — shows how each degrades
Thesis sentence"Fine-tuned AST outperforms CNN baselines on GTZAN genre classification, with X% accuracy, suggesting attention-based models capture long-range timbral patterns better than local convolutional features."

Music mood detection — predict valence and arousal from audio
Difficulty 2/5 DEAM dataset Psychology angle Regression task
Easy — psychologically rich
Predict the emotional valence (positive/negative) and arousal (calm/excited) of music clips using the circumplex model of affect. Bridges music, psychology, and ML. Very interesting thesis because it's fundamentally different from classification — you predict a number, not a label.
Full details
DatasetDEAM — 1802 music excerpts (45-sec clips), annotated with continuous valence and arousal ratings by multiple annotators. Free.
Dataset linkcvml.unige.ch/databases/DEAM

FeaturesMFCCs, chroma, spectral centroid, zero crossing rate (all extracted with librosa)

MetricPearson correlation coefficient + RMSE between predicted and human-rated valence/arousal
Key conceptRussell's circumplex model of affect — gives your literature review a solid psychological foundation
Thesis sentence"A CNN trained on mel spectrograms predicts music valence and arousal with r=X correlation on DEAM, outperforming handcrafted MFCC features, suggesting spectral representations better capture affective qualities of music."

Most novel

Beat tracking on Bangla folk music — evaluating Western models on Eastern rhythm
Most original
Difficulty 3/5 Genuinely novel Cultural advantage
Moderate — highly publishable
Existing beat trackers are trained almost entirely on Western music. Bangla folk and classical rhythms (taal cycles — Dadra, Kaharwa, Teentaal) are structurally different. Full details

📚 Priority Reading List — What to Read, When, and How Deeply
The honest truth about reading papers as a beginner
You do not need to understand every equation. Read the Abstract → Introduction → Results → Conclusion first. Only go into the methodology if you are implementing that exact approach. A paper you "skim well" is more useful than a paper you avoided because it looked hard.
How to read a paper — the 3-pass method
Pass 1 · 10 minSkim: Read title, abstract, intro first paragraph, all headings, conclusion. Answer: What problem? What did they do? Did it work? This is enough for papers you only need to cite.
Pass 2 · 30 minRead: Read intro fully, figures and tables, results, conclusion. Skip the math. Answer: How did they do it? What dataset? What metric? What result? Do this for your 5 core papers.
Pass 3 · 2 hrsStudy: Read every section including methodology and equations. Take notes. Answer: Could you implement this? Do this only for the 1–2 papers whose exact method you are replicating.

📅 Reading Schedule Mapped to Your 6-Month Plan
Month 1No papers yet. Focus entirely on Python and NumPy. Reading papers before you understand basic ML is overwhelming and counterproductive.
Month 2Read Velardo blog posts alongside his YouTube videos. Skim the PANNs paper abstract so you know what's coming. That's it.
Month 3 · Week 1🔴 Red papers only — your 2 "read fully" papers for your chosen topic. Block 3 hrs Saturday + 3 hrs Sunday. Take handwritten notes: What problem? What method? What result? What gap remains?
Month 3 · Week 2🟠 Orange paper — read before coding starts in Month 4. Focus on methodology and experimental setup sections. Note down: what hyperparameters? What train/val/test split? What metric?
Month 3 · Week 3🟡 Yellow papers — skim in 1 weekday session each. Abstract + results table + conclusion. Add to Zotero. One sentence note: "This paper did X and got Y — I cite it because Z."
Month 3 · Week 4⬜ Cite-only papers — abstract only, add to Zotero, move on. Do not spend more than 10 min per paper here.
Month 4–5No new papers unless you hit a specific problem that needs a citation. If you find a bug, search for a paper that describes the issue — don't browse randomly.
Month 6During writing, you may realise you need 1–2 more citations for specific claims. That's normal — search for them then and add to Zotero.
The biggest mistake BSc students make with papers

Reading too many papers in Month 3 and running out of time for Month 4. You need 5 papers maximum for a BSc thesis literature review. 2 you read fully, 2 you skim, 1 dataset paper. That is enough. Quality of understanding beats quantity of citations every time.

How to Access IEEE Papers via University WiFi
Step-by-step access instructions
Step 1
Connect to university WiFi on your phone or laptop. If you are off-campus, connect via your university's VPN first (ask your IT department for the VPN link — it's free for students).
Step 2
Open your browser and go to ieeexplore.ieee.org. You should see "Institutional Sign In" or your university name in the top right — that means access is active.
Step 3
In the search bar, paste the DOI (e.g. 10.1109/TSA.2002.800560) or the paper title. Hit Enter.
Step 4
On the paper page, click "Full Text PDF" or "Download PDF". It will open directly — no payment needed while on university WiFi.
If blocked
Search the paper title on scholar.google.com → click "All versions" → look for a free arxiv or ResearchGate link. Every paper below has a free preprint somewhere.
Save papers
Download to a folder named thesis/papers/. Add each one to Zotero (free) — it auto-generates your bibliography in IEEE or APA format.
How to cite in your thesis
For IEEE format: [1] G. Tzanetakis and P. Cook, "Musical genre classification of audio signals," IEEE Trans. Speech Audio Process., vol. 10, no. 5, pp. 293–302, Jul. 2002. — Use Zotero to generate this automatically. Never type references by hand.
